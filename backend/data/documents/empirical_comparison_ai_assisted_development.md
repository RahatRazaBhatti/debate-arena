SOURCE: arXiv (peer-reviewed case study)
TITLE: Developer Productivity With and Without GitHub Copilot: A Longitudinal Mixed-Methods Case Study
AUTHOR/ORGANIZATION: Viktoria Stray, Elias Goldmann Brandtzaeg, Viggo Tellefsen Wivestad, Astri Barbala, Nils Brede Moe (University of Oslo / SINTEF)
DATE: September 2025
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.20353
TOPIC: Empirical studies comparing AI-assisted and traditional development, AI-assisted developer productivity

CONTENT:
Most controlled studies of AI coding tools run in a lab, over a short
task, with volunteers who know they are being observed. This study
instead examined real, everyday engineering work at NAV IT, a large
Norwegian public-sector agile organization, over a two-year period. The
researchers analyzed 26,317 non-merge commits across 703 GitHub
repositories, comparing 25 developers who adopted GitHub Copilot
(introduced in September 2023) against 14 developers who did not,
supplemented by surveys and 13 semi-structured interviews.

The headline finding complicates a simple "AI makes developers more
productive" narrative: developers who used Copilot were already more
active (measured by commit-based activity) than non-users even BEFORE
Copilot was introduced, and the study did not find a statistically
significant change in commit-based activity for Copilot users after they
started using the tool, though the researchers noted small increases.
In other words, raw commit activity alone did not show a clear causal
productivity jump from adoption.

However, the survey and interview data told a more positive story:
developers' own PERCEIVED productivity increased notably after adopting
Copilot, and interviewees described real qualitative benefits -
generating boilerplate and repetitive code faster, less time spent
searching for syntax or examples, and reduced context-switching. The
authors explicitly caution that commit counts and lines of code are
known to be poor proxies for real engineering productivity (a point long
established in software engineering research), and that the gap between
perceived and measured productivity deserves further study - a pattern
also seen, in a different form, in METR's 2025 field study.

RELEVANCE: A genuinely empirical, real-workplace comparison of AI-assisted
versus traditional development, directly matching the "empirical studies
comparing AI-assisted and traditional development" category. Useful for
either side: it can support AI's productivity benefits (perceived gains,
qualitative time savings) or AI's limitations (no statistically
significant measured activity change) depending on which data the
argument leans on.
